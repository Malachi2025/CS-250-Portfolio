## Project Overview: SNHU Travel

The SNHU Travel project was built by a Scrum-Agile team working in sprints. I took on the tester role myself, without being assigned to it, to help make sure each user story met its acceptance criteria before we called it done.

What stood out to me most was how the team communicated. Daily stand-ups, sprint reviews, and retrospectives kept everyone on the same page and helped us adjust quickly when requirements changed. Because of that flexibility and the constant feedback, I believe Scrum-Agile was the best fit for SNHU Travel.

## Reflection (Module 8)

### How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How does creating user stories help with this?

I start by listening to the people who will actually use the product and turning what they say into small, specific pieces of work. On SNHU Travel, the Product Owner ran a focus group, and its feedback became stories like "As an end user, I want to click a link to view the top five destinations list so that I can see the most popular locations for travel." User stories help because they state who needs something, what they need, and why, and their acceptance criteria tell the developer what to build and the tester exactly what to check.

### How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I hope to bring into my future development work?

I like to build in short loops: deliver a small piece that works, get feedback, and adjust. When SNHU Travel pivoted to wellness travel, we reused the working Slide Show and swapped its content instead of starting over. Going forward I want to keep using a prioritized Product Backlog, Backlog Refinement to tighten vague stories, Daily Scrums to surface blockers early, and Sprint Reviews and Retrospectives to show working software and improve how the team works.

### What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?

A good team member communicates clearly, takes initiative, and is honest about what they can deliver. When no one assigned me a role, I took on the tester role myself. The most useful messages on our team restated decisions plainly, gave each person a next step, and invited questions. Being a good teammate also means pushing back respectfully when something is unclear, the way our tester did with vague stories, so the team builds the right thing.
